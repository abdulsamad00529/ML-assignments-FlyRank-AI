Research Question Framing
1. Proposed Research Title
Visual Intelligence for Quality Assurance in Online Fashion Retail: An AI-Driven System for Detecting Product Defects and Authenticity Risk from Customer-Submitted Images
(Working short title: "AI-Powered Visual QA and Authenticity Screening for Fashion E-Commerce")
2. Research Problem
Online fashion and luxury goods retailers face a persistent and costly problem: verifying, at scale, whether a product image submitted during a return, a dispute, or a quality complaint actually reflects a genuine defect, wear-and-tear, or an authenticity concern. This process is still largely manual. A support agent opens a ticket, looks at one or two photographs, and makes a judgment call based on experience and intuition. As order volumes grow, this manual review does not scale, and inconsistency between agents becomes a real business risk — two agents can look at the same image and reach different conclusions.
This problem matters because returns and disputes are one of the largest hidden cost centers in e-commerce, particularly in fashion, where subjective quality judgments (stitching defects, colour mismatch, fabric damage, counterfeit indicators) are common grounds for dispute. Small and mid-sized brands are affected the most, since they typically cannot afford large dedicated quality-assurance teams the way larger retailers can, yet they carry the same reputational exposure when a defective or counterfeit item slips through. Customers are affected as well: inconsistent or slow resolution erodes trust and, in the case of luxury goods, can create doubt about the legitimacy of the platform itself.
A concrete real-world parallel is how platforms such as StockX or Grailed rely on structured authentication pipelines for sneakers and luxury items, precisely because manual, ad-hoc review does not scale and does not build customer confidence. Smaller fashion e-commerce operations rarely have access to this kind of infrastructure, which is the gap this research is positioned to address.
3. Search / Discoverability Question
Primary research question: To what extent can a computer-vision-based classification system, trained on customer- and seller-submitted product images, reliably detect visual indicators of product defects and flag items with elevated authenticity risk, in order to support faster and more consistent return and dispute decisions in fashion e-commerce?
This question is oriented toward detection and decision support rather than open-ended exploration. It is measurable through standard classification metrics (precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion-matrix analysis across defect categories) and is scoped narrowly enough to be answerable within a capstone timeline.
4. Unit of Analysis
The unit of analysis is the individual product image submission, i.e., a single photograph (or a small, fixed-size set of photographs, e.g., 3–5 images) associated with one return/dispute event for one product unit.
This is the correct unit of analysis because:
The decision being modelled (approve/flag/escalate a return) is made per submission, not per customer or per product line.
Images, rather than videos or sensor logs, are the primary evidence type already collected by e-commerce platforms during the returns process, so no new data-collection infrastructure is required.
Framing the unit at the level of the transaction/event (rather than the product SKU) allows the system to account for condition-specific variation — the same SKU can arrive in different physical states across different customers.
5. Expected Output
For each submitted image set, the system will produce:
A defect classification (e.g., no visible defect, stitching/seam issue, fabric damage, colour/print inconsistency, packaging mismatch).
An authenticity risk score (a continuous value or a low/medium/high band) indicating the likelihood that visual features are inconsistent with genuine product references.
A short model-generated explanation (e.g., highlighted image regions or a brief natural-language rationale) supporting the classification, to make the output auditable rather than a black-box label.
This combination is useful because a single label is rarely sufficient for a business decision; a risk score allows prioritisation (which cases need urgent human review), while the explanation component gives the reviewing agent something to verify against, rather than asking them to trust the model blindly.
6. Decision
Based on the system's output, a customer-support or quality-assurance agent (or, in a fully automated pipeline, a rules layer built on top of the model) decides whether a returned or disputed item should be auto-approved, auto-rejected, or escalated for manual human review.
7. Action
Depending on the decision, the practical action taken is one of the following:
Auto-approve the return/refund and trigger the standard refund workflow without human intervention, for high-confidence, low-risk cases.
Auto-reject or request additional evidence, for cases where the image clearly does not support a defect claim.
Escalate to a human reviewer with the system's output attached as supporting context, for medium- or high-risk cases, so that the agent's time is focused only where it is genuinely needed.
8. Cost of a Wrong Recommendation
The cost of an incorrect output is asymmetric and needs to be discussed carefully:
False negative (a genuine defect or counterfeit indicator is missed and the system recommends approval as "no issue"): this creates direct financial loss (refunding or reselling a defective/counterfeit item), damages brand reputation if the item resurfaces, and, for luxury goods specifically, can expose the retailer to legal or contractual risk with suppliers.
False positive (a genuine, undamaged product is incorrectly flagged as defective or suspicious): this results in unnecessary escalation, slower resolution for a legitimate customer, added workload for human reviewers, and a poor customer experience that could reduce trust in the platform.
Operational risk: if the system is over-trusted and under-monitored, small systematic errors can compound at scale far faster than a human reviewer's errors would.
Ethical and trust considerations: an authenticity or defect flag is a judgment about a customer's honesty, not merely a product attribute. An incorrect flag can feel accusatory to the customer, which means the system's outputs should be treated as a decision-support signal, not a final verdict, especially in the early stages of deployment.
Because of this asymmetry, accuracy alone is not a sufficient evaluation criterion; the balance between false positive and false negative rates, and the threshold used to route cases to auto-decision versus human review, matters more than a single aggregate accuracy figure.
9. Why Machine Learning or AI Is Needed
A rule-based system (for example, simple image-quality heuristics or fixed thresholds on colour histograms) is insufficient here because defect and authenticity indicators are highly varied in appearance — a stitching defect on a jacket looks nothing like a stitching defect on a handbag, and counterfeit indicators are often subtle, inconsistent, and deliberately designed to resemble genuine features. These are not patterns that can be captured through a short list of fixed rules; they require learning visual features directly from labelled examples across many product categories and defect types.
Humans are also not a reliable baseline for consistency at scale. Individual agents differ in experience, attention, and fatigue over the course of a shift, which is why the same evidence can lead to different outcomes depending on who reviews it. A data-driven model, by contrast, applies the same learned decision boundary consistently across all submissions, which is valuable even before considering speed.
The value AI provides over traditional software, then, is not primarily "speed" — it is consistency of judgment across a highly variable visual domain, combined with the ability to improve as more labelled examples are collected, which a static rule-based system cannot do on its own.
10. Data Requirements
Required datasets: A labelled set of product images tagged with (a) defect category or "no defect," and (b) authenticity status where available (genuine vs. flagged-as-suspicious, if such labels can be sourced). Given the sensitivity of authenticity data, this component may need to be scoped as a proxy task (e.g., detecting visual inconsistency versus a reference catalogue image) rather than a true counterfeit-detection task, unless a partner dataset with verified labels is available.
Features: Raw image pixels processed through a convolutional or transformer-based vision backbone; optionally, metadata features such as product category, submitted reason code, and reference catalogue image for comparison.
Labels: Defect type (categorical), defect present/absent (binary), and, where feasible, authenticity risk band (ordinal: low/medium/high).
Data sources: A combination of (a) publicly available fashion product datasets for pretraining/transfer learning (e.g., DeepFashion, Fashion-MNIST for early prototyping, or fashion product image datasets available on Kaggle), and (b) a smaller, purpose-collected or simulated set of "defect" images (e.g., deliberately staged damaged garments) to fine-tune the model for the actual task, since large-scale labelled defect/authenticity datasets are not widely public.
Approximate dataset size: For a capstone-scale project, a fine-tuning set of roughly 2,000–5,000 labelled images across 4–6 defect categories is a realistic and defensible target, supplemented by transfer learning from a larger pretrained fashion dataset.
Data quality considerations: Image resolution and lighting consistency, class imbalance (defect-free images will vastly outnumber defect images in any real-world sample), and the risk of label noise since "defect" judgments are themselves somewhat subjective and may need a documented labelling rubric to keep annotations consistent.
Public datasets exist for general fashion product classification, but a dedicated public dataset for defect/authenticity detection is not readily available, which is an important limitation to state explicitly rather than assume away.
11. Assumptions
It is assumed that customers or sellers submit at least one usable, reasonably clear image per return/dispute case, consistent with existing return-request workflows.
It is assumed that a defensible labelling rubric can be developed in collaboration with a mentor or domain reference (e.g., existing QA guidelines), even if a large expert-labelled dataset is not available.
It is assumed that the authenticity component of this research will be framed conservatively, as a visual-inconsistency detector rather than a legally definitive counterfeit determination, given the sensitivity and liability associated with authenticity claims.
It is assumed that the system will operate as a decision-support tool integrated into an existing human review workflow, not as a fully autonomous decision-maker, at least during the scope of this capstone.
12. Limitations
The present scope is limited to static image analysis; it does not incorporate video evidence, physical inspection, or supplementary documentation (e.g., purchase receipts) that a human reviewer might also use. The authenticity-detection component is likely to be constrained by the scarcity of verified counterfeit-labelled data, meaning results in that specific area should be interpreted cautiously and framed as exploratory rather than conclusive. The model's performance may also vary considerably across product categories with very different visual characteristics (e.g., footwear versus jewellery), and a single unified model may underperform relative to category-specific models — a trade-off that future iterations could revisit. Finally, since defect-free images will substantially outnumber defect images in any realistically collected dataset, class imbalance will likely require targeted handling (e.g., weighted loss functions or data augmentation) rather than being treated as a minor implementation detail.
13. Why This Is More Than "Training a Model"
This research is not simply an exercise in training an image classifier; it is the design of an end-to-end intelligent decision-support system, and each stage carries its own set of design decisions:
Problem understanding: identifying that the real business problem is inconsistent, unscalable manual review, not "we need a computer vision model."
Data collection: sourcing, cleaning, and labelling images with an explicit rubric, given the absence of a ready-made public dataset for this exact task.
Feature engineering: deciding how to represent product category, comparison against a reference catalogue image, and metadata alongside raw visual features.
Model selection: choosing an appropriate architecture and evaluating trade-offs between transfer learning and training from scratch given the limited fine-tuning data.
Evaluation: assessing not just overall accuracy but the false-positive/false-negative trade-off described in Section 8, since these carry different real-world costs.
Risk assessment: explicitly reasoning about the asymmetric cost of errors and the ethical weight of an authenticity flag.
Human decision-making: designing the system to route cases to human review rather than replacing human judgment outright, particularly for medium- and high-risk cases.
Real-world deployment considerations: thinking through how such a system would need to be integrated into an existing customer-support workflow, including how agents would interact with and potentially override the system's recommendation.
Taken together, these stages position the project as a decision-support system embedded in a business process, with the model itself being only one component among several.
14. Future Scope
Future work may extend this research along several directions. The defect-detection component could be broadened to include video-based inspection (e.g., a short unboxing clip) rather than static images alone, which may capture texture and movement cues that a single photograph cannot. The authenticity component could be strengthened considerably if a partnership with a brand or verified reseller made a properly labelled counterfeit/genuine dataset available, allowing the proxy "visual inconsistency" task to be replaced with a more direct authenticity classification. The system could also be extended to actively request a specific follow-up photograph (e.g., "please photograph the inner label") when the initial submission is inconclusive, moving from passive classification toward an interactive verification process. Finally, longer-term work could explore how agent override decisions (cases where a human reviewer disagrees with the system's flag) could be fed back into the model as a continuous learning signal, so that the system's decision boundary improves over time based on real operational feedback rather than a fixed, one-time training set.
